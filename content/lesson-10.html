10.1Torque

An astronaut loosening a bolt on a spacewalk quickly learns three things: push far from the bolt, push perpendicular to the wrench, and pushing along the handle does nothing. The quantity that captures all three is torque, the tendency of a force to change rotation about a chosen axis. If \(\vec{r}\) runs from the axis to the point where \(\vec{F}\) acts and \(\phi\) is the angle between them, the moment arm (perpendicular distance from the axis to the force's line of action) is \(l = r\sin\phi\), and

\[\tau = Fl = rF\sin\phi = F_{tan}\,r.\]

Torque is measured in N·m (never joules) and always depends on the chosen axis: a force whose line of action passes through the axis has zero torque about it. Counterclockwise torques are positive.

As a vector, \(\vec{\tau} = \vec{r}\times\vec{F}\), perpendicular to both \(\vec{r}\) and \(\vec{F}\), with direction given by the right-hand rule. For vectors in the \(xy\) plane, \(\tau_z = xF_y - yF_x\), which supplies the sign automatically. Torques are everywhere in astronomy: attitude thrusters are placed far from a spacecraft's center of mass to get large torques from small thrusts; the Moon's pull on Earth's tidal bulges is slowly lengthening our day; and the Sun and Moon tugging on Earth's equatorial bulge make its axis precess.

Key equations
\[ \tau = Fl = rF\sin\phi \]
\[ \vec{\tau} = \vec{r}\times\vec{F} \]
\[ \tau_z = xF_y - yF_x \]

10.2Torque and Angular Acceleration for a Rigid Body

Apply Newton's second law to each particle of a rigid body: \(F_{i,tan} = m_i a_{i,tan} = m_i r_i\alpha\). Multiplying by \(r_i\) and summing, internal torques cancel in pairs and we get the rotational analog of Newton's second law:

\[\sum\tau_z = I\alpha_z.\]

Torque replaces force, moment of inertia replaces mass, and angular acceleration replaces acceleration. All torques and \(I\) must be about the same axis, and \(\alpha\) must be in rad/s2. This is why a reaction wheel's mass is concentrated in a heavy rim: a large \(I\) responds gently and precisely to its motor's torque.

In a uniform gravitational field, the total gravitational torque acts as if the whole weight acted at the center of mass. A telescope balanced on its axis therefore feels no gravitational torque, so a small motor can drive it.

Many problems mix rotation and translation, for example a winch at a lunar base lowering a payload into a crater. Draw a separate free-body diagram for each body: \(\sum F = ma\) for the payload, \(\sum\tau = I\alpha\) for the drum, and link them with \(a = R\alpha\) if the cable does not slip. For a solid drum (\(I = \tfrac12 MR^2\)) the payload accelerates at \(a = g/(1 + M/2m)\), always less than \(g\): the drum stores part of the energy as rotation.

Key equations
\[ \sum\tau_z = I\alpha_z \]
\[ a = R\alpha \]
\[ a = \frac{g}{1 + M/2m}\;(\text{solid-drum winch}) \]

10.3Rigid-Body Rotation About a Moving Axis

Rover wheels roll across Jezero Crater, and boulders shaken loose by moonquakes roll down crater walls. Every motion of a rigid body is a translation of the center of mass plus a rotation about the center of mass, so

\[K = \tfrac12 Mv_{cm}^2 + \tfrac12 I_{cm}\omega^2.\]

In rolling without slipping, the contact point is momentarily at rest, so \(v_{cm} = R\omega\) and the top of the wheel moves at \(2v_{cm}\). Mars rover drivers watch for violations of this condition: if the wheels turn more than the rover advances, it is slipping in sand (the fate of Spirit in 2009).

Writing \(I_{cm} = cMR^2\) (\(c\) = 2/5 solid sphere, 1/2 solid cylinder, 2/3 thin shell, 1 hoop), \(K = \tfrac12(1+c)Mv_{cm}^2\). Static friction at the contact point does no work, so energy is conserved for a body rolling from rest down a height \(h\):

\[v_{cm} = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+c}}.\]

The result is independent of mass and radius: only the shape factor \(c\) matters, and bodies with mass far from the center arrive more slowly. Using dynamics instead (\(\sum F = Ma_{cm}\) and \(\sum\tau = I_{cm}\alpha\) about the center of mass), a body rolling down a slope of angle \(\beta\) has \(a_{cm} = g\sin\beta/(1+c)\). The needed friction must not exceed \(\mu_s n\), or the body slides as well as rolls.

Key equations
\[ K = \tfrac12 Mv_{cm}^2 + \tfrac12 I_{cm}\omega^2 \]
\[ v_{cm} = R\omega \]
\[ v_{cm} = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+c}} \]
\[ a_{cm} = \frac{g\sin\beta}{1+c} \]

10.4Work and Power in Rotational Motion

An observatory dome is turned by motors pushing tangentially at its rim. When the dome turns through \(d\theta\), the point of application moves \(ds = R\,d\theta\), so the work is \(dW = F_{tan}R\,d\theta = \tau_z\,d\theta\). For a constant torque, \(W = \tau_z\Delta\theta\) (angle in radians), the rotational analog of \(W = F_x\Delta x\).

Using \(\tau_z = I\,d\omega_z/dt\) gives the work-energy theorem for rotation: the total work done by all torques equals the change in rotational kinetic energy, \(W_{tot} = \tfrac12 I\omega_2^2 - \tfrac12 I\omega_1^2\).

Dividing \(dW\) by \(dt\) gives the power, \(P = \tau_z\omega_z\), the analog of \(P = Fv\). At fixed power a motor can give large torque at low speed or small torque at high speed, which is why telescope drives use heavy gearing to deliver a large torque at the slow tracking rate.

Pulsars are flywheels losing power. Their magnetic fields and particle winds exert a braking torque, and from the measured period \(T\) and its slow increase \(dT/dt\) we get the spin-down power

\[P_{rot} = -\frac{dK}{dt} = \frac{4\pi^2 I\,(dT/dt)}{T^3}.\]

For the Crab pulsar this is about \(4\times10^{31}\) W, enough to make the whole Crab Nebula glow from radio to gamma rays.

Key equations
\[ W = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\tau_z\,d\theta \]
\[ W_{tot} = \tfrac12 I\omega_2^2 - \tfrac12 I\omega_1^2 \]
\[ P = \tau_z\omega_z \]
\[ P_{rot} = \frac{4\pi^2 I\,(dT/dt)}{T^3} \]

10.5Angular Momentum

The rotational analog of linear momentum is angular momentum. For a particle at position \(\vec{r}\) with momentum \(\vec{p}\),

\[\vec{L} = \vec{r}\times\vec{p} = \vec{r}\times m\vec{v},\qquad L = mvr\sin\phi = mvl,\]

with unit kg·m2/s. Like torque, it depends on the chosen origin. A spacecraft coasting in a straight line past a planet has constant \(L = mvl\) about the planet; a planet in a circular orbit has \(L = mvr\).

Differentiating \(\vec{L}\) gives \(\sum\vec{\tau} = d\vec{L}/dt\): net torque equals the rate of change of angular momentum. For any system, rigid or not (a galaxy, a gas cloud, an astronaut), internal torques cancel and only external torques change the total \(\vec{L}\).

For a rigid body spinning about a symmetry axis, \(\vec{L} = I\vec{\omega}\). With constant \(I\) this gives back \(\sum\tau = I\alpha\), but \(\sum\vec{\tau} = d\vec{L}/dt\) is more general and still works when \(I\) changes. Planets carry spin angular momentum \(I\omega\) and orbital angular momentum \(mvr\); for Earth the orbital part is millions of times larger. A great puzzle of solar system formation is that the Sun has 99.8% of the mass but less than 1% of the angular momentum, most of which is in the orbits of Jupiter and Saturn.

Key equations
\[ \vec{L} = \vec{r}\times m\vec{v} \]
\[ \sum\vec{\tau} = \frac{d\vec{L}}{dt} \]
\[ \vec{L} = I\vec{\omega} \]
\[ \Delta L = \tau\,\Delta t \]

10.6Conservation of Angular Momentum

If the net external torque on a system is zero, its total angular momentum is conserved. This holds even for non-rigid systems. For a body whose moment of inertia changes while it spins about a fixed axis, \(I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2\): pull mass inward and the spin speeds up.

An astronaut floating in the space station who pulls in her arms spins faster; extending them slows her down. Kinetic energy is not conserved: \(K = L^2/2I\) increases as \(I\) decreases, and the extra energy comes from the work of her muscles.

On stellar scales, gravity points toward the center and exerts no torque about it, so a collapsing core keeps its angular momentum. For a uniform sphere of fixed mass, \(R^2\omega\) is constant, so the period scales as

\[T_2 = T_1\left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^2.\]

Shrinking from the Sun's size to a 12 km neutron star cuts the period by a factor of more than \(3\times10^9\), which is why newborn neutron stars spin many times per second.

The same law shapes planetary systems. A slowly rotating interstellar cloud spins faster as it collapses. Material can fall freely along the axis but not toward it, so it flattens into a rotating protoplanetary disk like the one ALMA imaged around HL Tauri. Our planets still orbit in nearly one plane and one direction, a fossil of that disk.

Key equations
\[ \sum\vec{\tau}_{ext} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \vec{L} = \text{constant} \]
\[ I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2 \]
\[ T_2 = T_1\left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^2 \]
\[ K = \frac{L^2}{2I} \]

10.7Gyroscopes and Precession

A spinning top traces slow circles instead of falling, and Earth's axis slowly sweeps around the sky: 4600 years ago the pole star was Thuban, and in about 12,000 years it will be near Vega. This is precession, the slow rotation of a spin axis under a torque.

For a gyroscope supported at one end of a horizontal axle, the weight \(w\) acting a distance \(r\) from the pivot gives a horizontal torque \(\tau = wr\), perpendicular to the spin angular momentum \(\vec{L}\). Since \(d\vec{L} = \vec{\tau}\,dt\) is perpendicular to \(\vec{L}\), it changes the direction of \(\vec{L}\), not its size, so the axle swings around horizontally at the precession angular speed

\[\Omega = \frac{\tau}{L} = \frac{wr}{I\omega}.\]

Faster spin means slower precession; the theory assumes \(\Omega \ll \omega\). For an axis tilted at angle \(\theta\) to the precession axis, \(\tau = \Omega L\sin\theta\).

Spinning rotors resist changes in direction, so they make excellent references: Hubble senses its rotations with gyroscopes, and Juno spins to keep its antenna pointed at Earth. Gravity Probe B's ultra-round quartz gyroscopes confirmed general relativity's tiny geodetic (6.6 arcsec/yr) and frame-dragging (0.039 arcsec/yr) precessions. Earth itself is a gyroscope: the Sun and Moon pull on its equatorial bulge, tilted 23.4° to the ecliptic, and the resulting torque makes the axis precess once every 25,800 years, the precession of the equinoxes that Hipparchus discovered around 130 BCE.

Key equations
\[ \Omega = \frac{\tau}{L} = \frac{wr}{I\omega} \]
\[ \tau = \Omega L\sin\theta \]
\[ T_p = \frac{2\pi}{\Omega} \]

★Key takeaways

  • Torque \(\tau = rF\sin\phi\) (or \(\vec{\tau} = \vec{r}\times\vec{F}\)) measures a force's turning effect about a chosen axis; forces through the axis give zero torque.
  • For a rigid body, \(\sum\tau_z = I\alpha_z\); combine it with \(\sum F = ma\) and \(a = R\alpha\) for systems like winches.
  • Rolling without slipping means \(v_{cm} = R\omega\); a body rolling from rest down height \(h\) reaches \(v = \sqrt{2gh/(1+c)}\), independent of its mass and size.
  • Rotational work is \(W = \tau\Delta\theta\) and power is \(P = \tau\omega\); a pulsar's spin-down power can light a whole nebula.
  • Net torque changes angular momentum, \(\sum\vec{\tau} = d\vec{L}/dt\); with no external torque \(L = I\omega\) is conserved, so collapsing stars and clouds spin up.
  • A torque perpendicular to a spinning body's \(\vec{L}\) makes it precess at \(\Omega = \tau/L\), as with gyroscopes and Earth's 25,800-year axial precession.

Example 10.4 A runaway winch on the Moon

A winch drum, a solid cylinder of mass 20.0 kg and radius 0.250 m, turns on a frictionless axle. Its cable supports an 8.00 kg instrument package. The brake fails and the package starts from rest. Lunar gravity is \(g_M = 1.62\) m/s2. Find (a) the package's acceleration, (b) the cable tension, (c) the drum's angular acceleration, and (d) the package's speed after it falls 2.00 m.

Show solution (try it first)

Identify and set up: The package translates and the drum rotates, linked by \(a = R\alpha\). Take downward as positive for the package and the drum's turning direction as positive for the drum. The drum has \(I = \tfrac12 MR^2\), and only the tension \(T\) has a torque about the axle.

Execute: Package: \(mg_M - T = ma\). Drum: \(TR = I\alpha = \tfrac12 MR^2(a/R)\), so \(T = \tfrac12 Ma\). Combining, \(a = g_M/(1 + M/2m)\). (a) \(a = 1.62/[1 + 20.0/16.0] = 1.62/2.25 = 0.720\) m/s2. (b) \(T = m(g_M - a) = (8.00)(1.62 - 0.720) = 7.20\) N. (c) \(\alpha = a/R = 0.720/0.250 = 2.88\) rad/s2. (d) \(v = \sqrt{2ay} = \sqrt{2(0.720)(2.00)} = 1.70\) m/s.

Evaluate: The tension (7.20 N) is less than the package's lunar weight (13.0 N), as it must be for the package to accelerate downward. A massless drum (\(M \to 0\)) would give free fall, \(a = g_M\). The ratio \(a/g\) depends only on \(M/m\), so on Earth the same winch would give \(9.80/2.25 = 4.36\) m/s2.

Example 10.5 A boulder rolls down a lunar crater wall

A roughly spherical boulder of radius 0.500 m, dislodged by a moonquake, rolls without slipping from rest down a crater wall, dropping a vertical height of 15.0 m. Treat it as a uniform solid sphere and use \(g = 1.62\) m/s2. (a) What is its speed at the bottom? (b) What is its angular speed there? (c) What fraction of its kinetic energy is rotational?

Show solution (try it first)

Identify and set up: Rolling without slipping conserves mechanical energy. For a solid sphere \(c = 2/5\), so \(v_{cm} = \sqrt{2gh/(1+c)}\), and \(\omega = v_{cm}/R\).

Execute: (a) \(v_{cm} = \sqrt{10gh/7} = \sqrt{10(1.62)(15.0)/7} = 5.89\) m/s. (b) \(\omega = 5.89/0.500 = 11.8\) rad/s. (c) \(K_{rot}/K = c/(1+c) = (2/5)/(7/5) = 2/7 = 0.286\).

Evaluate: A frictionless slide would give \(\sqrt{2gh} = 6.97\) m/s; the rolling boulder is slower because 28.6% of its energy is tied up in spin. Neither mass nor radius appeared, so a pebble and a house-sized boulder of the same shape reach the bottom together.

Example 10.11 Spinning up a collapsed star

Suppose the Sun (radius \(7.00\times10^{5}\) km, rotation period 25.0 days) collapsed to a neutron star of radius 12.0 km without losing mass or angular momentum. Treating the Sun before and after as a uniform sphere, what would its new rotation period be, and how many revolutions per second would it make?

Show solution (try it first)

Identify and set up: Gravity is a central force with no torque about the center, so \(L\) is conserved. For uniform spheres of equal mass, \(T_2 = T_1(R_2/R_1)^2\).

Execute: \(T_1 = (25.0\text{ d})(86{,}400\text{ s/d}) = 2.16\times10^6\) s. Then \(T_2 = (2.16\times10^6\text{ s})[(12.0)/(7.00\times10^5)]^2 = 6.35\times10^{-4}\) s, or \(1/T_2 = 1.58\times10^3\) revolutions per second.

Evaluate: The period shrinks by a factor \((7.00\times10^5/12.0)^2 = 3.40\times10^9\). A spin of 1580 rev/s is faster than any known pulsar: real collapsing cores shed angular momentum to outer layers and magnetic fields, and stars are far from uniform. Still, the estimate shows why rapid spin is natural for neutron stars.
