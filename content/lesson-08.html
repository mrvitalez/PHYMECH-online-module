8.1Momentum and Impulse

Which is harder to stop: a half-kilogram pebble moving at 20 km/s, or a one-tonne rover creeping along at a few cm/s? Both mass and velocity matter, and the quantity that combines them is momentum, \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\). Momentum is a vector pointing along the velocity, with SI unit kg·m/s. Newton actually wrote his second law in terms of momentum: \(\sum \vec{F} = d\vec{p}/dt\). This form stays valid even when mass changes, as in a rocket.

The impulse of the net force over a time interval is \(\vec{J} = \int \sum\vec{F}\,dt\); for a constant force it is simply \(\sum\vec{F}\,\Delta t\). Its unit, N·s, equals kg·m/s, and that is no coincidence. The impulse-momentum theorem says \(\vec{J} = \vec{p}_2 - \vec{p}_1\): the change in momentum equals the impulse, however complicated the force. On a graph of force versus time, the impulse is the area under the curve, and the average force is \(\vec{F}_{av} = \vec{J}/\Delta t\).

This is why landers use crushable legs, airbags (Mars Pathfinder, Spirit, Opportunity) or a sky crane: the momentum to be removed is fixed, so stretching the stopping time lowers the force. Compare with kinetic energy \(K = p^2/2m\), a scalar: momentum changes depend on force × time, energy changes on force × distance. A tiny fast meteoroid can have modest momentum but enormous kinetic energy, which is what digs craters.

Key equations
\[ \vec{p} = m\vec{v} \]
\[ \sum\vec{F} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} \]
\[ \vec{J} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sum\vec{F}\,dt = \vec{p}_2 - \vec{p}_1 \]
\[ \vec{F}_{av} = \frac{\vec{J}}{\Delta t} \]

8.2Conservation of Momentum

Two astronauts floating at rest beside the space station push palm to palm and drift apart. Where did their momenta come from? During the push, the forces they exert on each other are internal forces: equal and opposite at every instant by Newton's third law. Adding \(d\vec{p}_A/dt\) and \(d\vec{p}_B/dt\), the internal forces cancel. In general, for the total momentum \(\vec{P} = \vec{p}_A + \vec{p}_B + \cdots\), only external forces can change it: \(\sum\vec{F}_{ext} = d\vec{P}/dt\).

This gives the principle of conservation of momentum: if the net external force on a system is zero, \(\vec{P}\) is constant. Internal forces, however violent (an explosion, a collision), can only move momentum from one part of the system to another. It is a vector law, so each component is conserved separately.

In astronomy gravity is everywhere, but momentum conservation still works whenever the interaction is brief or the internal forces dominate. When a comet nucleus splits, the fragments push apart in seconds while the Sun's pull changes their momenta only over days. The same idea explains recoil: an ion engine expels xenon backward and the probe moves forward; an asymmetric supernova gives its neutron star a kick that can exceed 1000 km/s. Always choose the system so that it includes both partners of every important interaction.

Key equations
\[ \vec{P} = \vec{p}_A + \vec{p}_B + \cdots \]
\[ \sum\vec{F}_{ext} = \frac{d\vec{P}}{dt} \]
\[ \sum\vec{F}_{ext} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \vec{P} = \text{constant} \]

8.3Momentum Conservation and Collisions

To an astronomer a collision is any strong, short interaction in which the forces between the bodies dwarf any external forces. Total momentum is the same just before and just after. Kinetic energy usually is not: some becomes heat, light, deformation, and ejecta. Such collisions are inelastic; if the bodies stick together, the collision is completely inelastic and the final velocity is

\[\vec{v}_2 = \frac{m_A\vec{v}_{A1} + m_B\vec{v}_{B1}}{m_A + m_B}.\]

With \(B\) initially at rest, \(K_2/K_1 = m_A/(m_A+m_B)\), always less than one. When a tiny projectile hits a huge target, almost all the kinetic energy turns into heat, as when fragments of Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 hit Jupiter at 60 km/s in 1994.

NASA's DART mission (2022) hit Dimorphos, a 160 m moon of asteroid Didymos, in a nearly completely inelastic collision. The resulting few mm/s change shortened Dimorphos's 11 h 55 min orbit by about 33 minutes. Ejecta blasted backward carried momentum too, so the asteroid got an extra push, described by a momentum enhancement factor \(\beta \approx 3\)-4. Planet formation is itself a chain of sticky collisions: planetesimals merge, their momenta add as vectors, and the 'lost' kinetic energy heats and melts the growing body.

Key equations
\[ \vec{v}_2 = \frac{m_A\vec{v}_{A1} + m_B\vec{v}_{B1}}{m_A + m_B} \]
\[ \frac{K_2}{K_1} = \frac{m_A}{m_A + m_B}\quad(v_{B1}=0) \]

8.4Elastic Collisions

In an elastic collision total kinetic energy is conserved as well as momentum. That needs conservative interaction forces, as in collisions between nuclei in a stellar plasma or gravitational encounters between a spacecraft and a planet. In one dimension, combining both conservation laws shows that the relative velocity simply reverses: \(v_{B2x} - v_{A2x} = -(v_{B1x} - v_{A1x})\). With \(B\) initially at rest,

\[v_{A2x} = \frac{m_A - m_B}{m_A + m_B}v_{A1x},\qquad v_{B2x} = \frac{2m_A}{m_A + m_B}v_{A1x}.\]

Three limits are worth remembering: equal masses swap velocities; a light body bounces off a heavy one with almost its original speed; a heavy body hitting a light one sends it off at nearly twice the heavy body's speed. Energy transfer is most efficient for equal masses, which is why hydrogen slows neutrons well, and why electrons and ions in a hot plasma exchange energy so slowly.

The gravitational slingshot is an elastic 'collision' with a planet. In the planet's frame the probe leaves with the same speed it arrived with. If the probe approaches head-on at speed \(v\) and the planet moves toward it at \(U\), a full U-turn sends it off at \(v + 2U\) in the Sun's frame. The planet slows by an immeasurably tiny amount. Voyager, Galileo, Cassini and New Horizons all used this trick.

Key equations
\[ v_{B2x} - v_{A2x} = -(v_{B1x} - v_{A1x}) \]
\[ v_{A2x} = \frac{m_A - m_B}{m_A + m_B}v_{A1x} \]
\[ v_{B2x} = \frac{2m_A}{m_A + m_B}v_{A1x} \]

8.5Center of Mass

The Moon does not simply orbit the Earth: both orbit a common point, the center of mass, which astronomers call the barycenter. It is the mass-weighted average position, \(\vec{r}_{cm} = \sum m_i\vec{r}_i / M\). For two bodies separated by \(d\), it lies on the line between them with \(m_1 r_1 = m_2 r_2\), so \(r_1 = m_2 d/(m_1 + m_2)\), closer to the heavier body.

Differentiating gives \(\vec{P} = M\vec{v}_{cm}\) and \(\sum\vec{F}_{ext} = M\vec{a}_{cm}\): the center of mass moves as if all the mass and all the external forces were concentrated there. When a comet fragments, the pieces scatter but their center of mass follows the original orbit.

The Earth-Moon barycenter is about 4700 km from Earth's center, still inside the Earth. The Sun-Jupiter barycenter lies just outside the Sun's surface. A star and its planet both circle their barycenter with zero total momentum, so \(m_\star v_\star = m_p v_p\). Measuring the star's tiny Doppler wobble therefore reveals the planet. In 1995 Mayor and Queloz found a 56 m/s wobble with a 4.23-day period in 51 Pegasi: the first planet around a Sun-like star, and a 2019 Nobel Prize. Jupiter makes the Sun wobble at about 12.5 m/s; Earth manages only 9 cm/s.

Key equations
\[ \vec{r}_{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i\vec{r}_i}{M} \]
\[ r_1 = \frac{m_2 d}{m_1 + m_2} \]
\[ \vec{P} = M\vec{v}_{cm} \]
\[ \sum\vec{F}_{ext} = M\vec{a}_{cm} \]
\[ m_\star v_\star = m_p v_p \]

8.6Rocket Propulsion

A rocket in deep space has nothing to push against: it moves forward by throwing propellant backward. If exhaust leaves at speed \(v_{ex}\) relative to the rocket, conserving momentum over a short time \(dt\) gives \(m\,dv = -v_{ex}\,dm\). The forward force on the rocket, the thrust, is

\[F = -v_{ex}\frac{dm}{dt} = v_{ex}\left|\frac{dm}{dt}\right|.\]

Large thrust needs a high exhaust speed or a high mass flow. Chemical engines reach 2.5-4.5 km/s; the ion engines on Dawn reached about 30 km/s but with a thrust like the weight of a sheet of paper. Since \(a = F/m\), a rocket accelerates faster as it gets lighter.

Integrating gives the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, \(\Delta v = v_{ex}\ln(m_0/m_f)\). Mission planners budget in 'delta-v': about 9.4 km/s to reach low Earth orbit, about 3.6 km/s more to head for Mars. The logarithm is cruel: doubling \(\Delta v\) means squaring the mass ratio. A single kerosene stage would need \(m_0/m_f \approx 23\), so 96% of the rocket would be propellant. That is why rockets use staging, dropping empty tanks and engines. When launching from a planet, the weight \(mg\) must also be subtracted from the thrust.

Key equations
\[ F = -v_{ex}\frac{dm}{dt} \]
\[ a = -\frac{v_{ex}}{m}\frac{dm}{dt} \]
\[ \Delta v = v_{ex}\ln\frac{m_0}{m_f} \]

★Key takeaways

  • Momentum \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\) is a vector; the impulse of the net force equals the change in momentum, so stretching a stopping time lowers the average force.
  • If the net external force on a system is zero, its total momentum is conserved, no matter how violent the internal forces are. Each component is conserved separately.
  • In every collision momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions. Completely inelastic (sticking) collisions lose the most kinetic energy.
  • In a 1D elastic collision the relative velocity reverses; a planetary flyby can add up to twice the planet's orbital speed to a spacecraft.
  • The center of mass (barycenter) moves as if all mass and external force acted there; a star's wobble \(m_\star v_\star = m_p v_p\) reveals unseen planets.
  • Rockets work by ejecting momentum: thrust is \(v_{ex}|dm/dt|\) and the total velocity change is \(\Delta v = v_{ex}\ln(m_0/m_f)\).

Example 8.2 Touchdown on Mars

A \(1.0\times10^{3}\) kg lander touches down vertically on Mars at 2.5 m/s and is brought to rest by crushable legs in 0.20 s. (a) What impulse does the net force deliver? (b) What is the average net force? (c) What is the average upward force from the ground, and how does it compare with the lander's weight on Mars (\(g_{Mars} = 3.71\) m/s2)?

Show solution (try it first)

Identify and set up: Use the impulse-momentum theorem. Take \(+y\) upward, so \(v_{1y} = -2.5\) m/s and \(v_{2y} = 0\). The net force is the ground force \(n\) minus the weight \(w = mg_{Mars}\).

Execute: (a) \(J_y = p_{2y} - p_{1y} = 0 - (1.0\times10^3\text{ kg})(-2.5\text{ m/s}) = +2.5\times10^3\) N·s. (b) \(F_{av,y} = J_y/\Delta t = (2.5\times10^3\text{ N·s})/(0.20\text{ s}) = 1.25\times10^4\) N. (c) The weight is \(w = (1.0\times10^3)(3.71) = 3.71\times10^3\) N, so \(n_{av} = F_{av,y} + w = 1.25\times10^4 + 3.71\times10^3 = 1.6\times10^4\) N, about 4.4 times the Martian weight.

Evaluate: The impulse points upward, opposite to the initial motion, as it must to stop the lander. Stopping in 0.020 s instead would make the force ten times larger, which is why landing systems are built to extend the stopping time.

Example 8.5 DART strikes Dimorphos

DART (mass 580 kg) struck Dimorphos at 6.14 km/s. Take the mass of Dimorphos as \(4.3\times10^{9}\) kg, initially at rest in a frame moving with its orbit. (a) Assuming a completely inelastic collision, what was Dimorphos's velocity change? (b) How much kinetic energy was converted to other forms? (c) If the momentum enhancement factor was \(\beta = 3.6\), what was the actual velocity change?

Show solution (try it first)

Identify and set up: The impact lasted far less than a second, so Didymos's gravity delivered negligible impulse and momentum is conserved. Take \(+x\) along DART's velocity and use \(v_2 = m_A v_{A1}/(m_A + m_B)\).

Execute: (a) \(v_2 = (580)(6.14\times10^3)/(4.3\times10^9 + 580) = 8.3\times10^{-4}\) m/s \(= 0.83\) mm/s. (b) \(K_1 = \tfrac12(580)(6.14\times10^3)^2 = 1.09\times10^{10}\) J, while \(K_2 = \tfrac12(4.3\times10^9)(8.28\times10^{-4})^2 \approx 1.5\times10^3\) J. Essentially all \(1.09\times10^{10}\) J (about 2.6 tonnes of TNT) became heat, fracture and ejecta. (c) \(\Delta v = \beta v_2 = (3.6)(0.83\text{ mm/s}) = 3.0\) mm/s.

Evaluate: A few mm/s sounds tiny, but Dimorphos orbits Didymos at only about 17 cm/s, so this is nearly a 2% change, enough to shorten its period by about half an hour. With decades of warning, a similar nudge could make a dangerous asteroid miss Earth.

Example 8.12 Propellant for a trip to Mars

A spacecraft in low Earth orbit must perform a trans-Mars injection burn with \(\Delta v = 3.6\) km/s. (a) What mass ratio and propellant fraction are needed with a hydrogen-oxygen engine (\(v_{ex} = 4.4\) km/s)? (b) Repeat for a kerosene engine (\(v_{ex} = 3.0\) km/s). (c) With the hydrogen engine, how much propellant is needed if the burnout mass is 25 tonnes?

Show solution (try it first)

Identify and set up: The burn is in orbit and brief, so use the rocket equation solved for the mass ratio, \(m_0/m_f = e^{\Delta v/v_{ex}}\). The propellant fraction is \(1 - m_f/m_0\).

Execute: (a) \(m_0/m_f = e^{3.6/4.4} = 2.27\), propellant fraction \(1 - 1/2.27 = 0.56\). (b) \(m_0/m_f = e^{3.6/3.0} = 3.32\), fraction \(1 - 1/3.32 = 0.70\). (c) \(m_{prop} = m_f(m_0/m_f - 1) = (2.5\times10^4\text{ kg})(2.27 - 1) = 3.2\times10^4\) kg.

Evaluate: With the better engine 56% of the departing spacecraft is propellant; with kerosene, 70%. Because the exhaust speed sits inside an exponential, a modest improvement saves a lot of mass, which is why deep-space upper stages so often burn liquid hydrogen.
