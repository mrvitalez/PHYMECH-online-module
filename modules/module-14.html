14.1Describing Oscillations

Every oscillating system has an equilibrium position and a restoring force that pushes it back when displaced. Because it returns to equilibrium still moving, it overshoots, and the cycle repeats. A Cepheid variable such as \(\delta\) Cephei literally pulsates: gas pressure pushes out, gravity pulls in, and the star overshoots on every swing.

The vocabulary:

  • Amplitude \(A\): the maximum displacement from equilibrium (always positive).
  • Period \(T\): the time for one complete cycle.
  • Frequency \(f = 1/T\): cycles per second, in hertz (1 Hz = 1 s\(^{-1}\)).
  • Angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi/T\), in rad/s.

Keep \(f\) and \(\omega\) distinct: the Crab pulsar has \(f = 29.7\) Hz but \(\omega = 186\) rad/s.

Periodic motion need not be sinusoidal; it only has to repeat. The pulses of the Crab pulsar (every 33.7 ms) and the eclipses of Algol (every 2.867 d) look nothing like sine waves, yet their periods carry physical information: a Cepheid's period reveals its luminosity, a pulsar's period its spin, and a binary's period (with Kepler's third law) the stars' masses. Astronomical periods range from milliseconds to years, and the Sun itself rings with millions of five-minute acoustic oscillations used in helioseismology.

Key equations
\[ f = \frac{1}{T} \]
\[ \omega = 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T} \]

14.2Simple Harmonic Motion

When the restoring force is proportional to the displacement, \(F_x = -kx\) (Hooke's law), the motion is simple harmonic motion (SHM), and \(a_x = -(k/m)x\).

SHM is the shadow of circular motion. Galileo saw Jupiter's moons slide back and forth along a line: we view their circular orbits edge on, and the projection \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) obeys exactly the SHM equation, with

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \]

The period of SHM does not depend on the amplitude: a stiffer spring gives a shorter period, a heavier body a longer one.

Differentiating gives the velocity and acceleration:

\[ v_x = -\omega A\sin(\omega t+\phi), \qquad a_x = -\omega^2 A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \]

The speed is greatest (\(\omega A\)) at equilibrium and zero at the turning points; the acceleration is always opposite to \(x\), largest (\(\omega^2A\)) at the turning points. The phase angle \(\phi\) follows from the initial conditions.

Weighing astronauts in orbit. A scale reads zero on the ISS, but \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\) depends on mass, not weight. The Body Mass Measurement Device rocks the astronaut on springs and times the oscillation.

Key equations
\[ F_x = -kx \]
\[ x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) \]
\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \quad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \]
\[ v_{max} = \omega A, \quad a_{max} = \omega^2 A \]

14.3Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion

The spring force is conservative, so the total mechanical energy is constant. At a turning point all the energy is potential, so

\[ E = \tfrac12 m v_x^2 + \tfrac12 k x^2 = \tfrac12 k A^2 \]

Energy sloshes between kinetic and potential forms twice per cycle. Solving for the speed at any position (no time needed):

\[ v_x = \pm\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\sqrt{A^2 - x^2} \]

On the energy diagram, \(U = \tfrac12 kx^2\) is a parabola and \(E\) a horizontal line; the gap between them is \(K\), and the body is confined between the turning points \(x = \pm A\). Kinetic and potential energies are equal at \(x = \pm A/\sqrt{2}\).

Why SHM is everywhere. Near any stable equilibrium, a smooth potential energy curve looks like a parabola, with effective stiffness \(k_{eff} = d^2U/dx^2\). So small oscillations about any minimum are simple harmonic: stars pulsate, moons librate and molecules vibrate nearly sinusoidally.

Vibration isolation. If an instrument on soft springs is kicked to speed \(v_0\) at equilibrium, energy conservation gives the amplitude \(A = v_0/\omega\), which engineers keep within safe limits.

Key equations
\[ E = \tfrac12 m v_x^2 + \tfrac12 k x^2 = \tfrac12 k A^2 \]
\[ v_x = \pm\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\sqrt{A^2 - x^2} \]

14.4Applications of Simple Harmonic Motion

Vertical SHM. A lander resting on spring legs compresses by \(\Delta l\) with \(k\Delta l = mg\). Measured from this new equilibrium, the net force is again \(-kx\), so \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\) is unchanged: gravity shifts the center of the oscillation, not its rate. Equivalently \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\Delta l/g}\).

Angular SHM. If a restoring torque obeys \(\tau_z = -\kappa\theta\), then \(\omega = \sqrt{\kappa/I}\) and \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{I/\kappa}\). Cavendish used such a torsion balance to measure \(G\).

Molecular vibrations. Small vibrations of a diatomic molecule are SHM with the reduced mass \(\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)\). Carbon monoxide vibrates at \(6.42\times10^{13}\) Hz, in the infrared, which lets telescopes like JWST trace cold star-forming clouds.

A tunnel through a planet. Inside a uniform planet only the enclosed mass pulls, so \(F_x = -(GMm/R^3)x\), a Hooke's-law force. A capsule dropped in a tunnel through the center oscillates with

\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{GM}} = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{G\rho}} \]

exactly the period of a satellite skimming the surface (about 108 min for the Moon), and it depends only on the planet's density.

Key equations
\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\Delta l}{g}} \]
\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{I}} \]
\[ \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1+m_2} \]
\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{GM}} \]

14.5The Simple Pendulum

A simple pendulum is a point mass on a massless string of length \(L\). The restoring force is the tangential component of gravity, \(F_\theta = -mg\sin\theta\). For small angles \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\) (error only 0.5% at 10\(^\circ\)), and with arc length \(s = L\theta\) the force is \(-(mg/L)s\): Hooke's law with \(k = mg/L\). Therefore

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \]

The mass cancels, for the same reason all bodies fall at the same rate. The period depends only on length and local gravity (it grows slightly at large amplitudes).

Pendulums on other worlds. Since \(T\propto 1/\sqrt{g}\), a pendulum is a portable gravimeter. A grandfather clock swings 2.46 times more slowly on the Moon and 1.63 times more slowly on Mars, which is why spacecraft use quartz and atomic clocks. Time many swings to reduce reaction-time errors, then use \(g = 4\pi^2L/T^2\).

The Foucault pendulum. In 1851 Foucault's pendulum in Paris slowly turned its plane of swing, a full turn in about 32 h, because the Earth rotated beneath it: the first laboratory demonstration of the Earth's rotation. At latitude \(\lambda\) the turn takes one sidereal day divided by \(\sin\lambda\).

Key equations
\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \]
\[ g = \frac{4\pi^2 L}{T^2} \]

14.6The Physical Pendulum

A physical pendulum is any rigid body swinging about a fixed axis that does not pass through its center of mass: an astronaut's leg, a meteorite hung on a pin, a robot arm. If the cm is a distance \(d\) from the pivot, the weight exerts a torque \(\tau_z = -mgd\sin\theta\approx -(mgd)\theta\). This is angular SHM with \(\kappa = mgd\), so

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{mgd}{I}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgd}} \]

where \(I\) is the moment of inertia about the pivot (use the parallel-axis theorem if needed). For a point mass, \(I = mL^2\) and \(d = L\) give back the simple pendulum.

Walking on the Moon. A leg is roughly a uniform rod pivoted at the hip, with \(I = \tfrac13 mL^2\), \(d = L/2\), so \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{2L/3g}\). On the Moon the natural swing takes 2.46 times longer, which is why Apollo astronauts preferred a bounding hop to walking.

Measuring moments of inertia. Turned around, \(I = mgdT^2/4\pi^2\). Engineers swing spacecraft from pivots before launch to measure their moments of inertia, which the attitude control system needs.

Key equations
\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgd}} \]
\[ I = \frac{mgdT^2}{4\pi^2} \]

14.7Damped Oscillations

Real oscillators lose energy to friction and drag, so their amplitude decreases: damping. With a damping force \(F_x = -bv_x\), a lightly damped oscillator moves as

\[ x = Ae^{-(b/2m)t}\cos(\omega' t + \phi), \qquad \omega' = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m} - \frac{b^2}{4m^2}} \]

The amplitude decays by a factor \(e\) in \(\tau = 2m/b\) (the energy, proportional to \(A^2\), in \(\tau/2\)), and the frequency is slightly lowered.

  • Underdamped, \(b < 2\sqrt{km}\): decaying oscillation.
  • Critically damped, \(b_c = 2\sqrt{km}\): returns to equilibrium fastest, without overshooting.
  • Overdamped, \(b > 2\sqrt{km}\): creeps back slowly.

Light damping is described by the quality factor \(Q = m\omega/b = \omega\tau/2\); the system rings for about \(Q/\pi\) cycles. The time for the amplitude to fall to a fraction \(F\) is \(\tau\ln(1/F)\).

Seismometers. A seismometer's proof mass is made nearly critically damped so it follows the ground faithfully instead of ringing. InSight's SEIS recorded over 1300 marsquakes. Apollo seismometers found that the dry lunar crust, with \(Q\) of several thousand, rings like a bell for more than an hour after an impact.

Key equations
\[ x = Ae^{-(b/2m)t}\cos(\omega' t+\phi) \]
\[ \omega' = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m} - \frac{b^2}{4m^2}} \]
\[ b_c = 2\sqrt{km} \]
\[ Q = \frac{m\omega}{b} = \frac{\omega\tau}{2}, \quad \tau = \frac{2m}{b} \]

14.8Forced Oscillations and Resonance

Driving a damped oscillator (natural frequency \(\omega\)) with a force \(F_{max}\cos\omega_d t\) makes it oscillate, after transients die away, at the driving frequency with amplitude

\[ A = \frac{F_{max}}{\sqrt{(k - m\omega_d^2)^2 + b^2\omega_d^2}} \]

At low \(\omega_d\), \(A\approx F_{max}/k\); at high \(\omega_d\) the inertia cannot keep up and \(A\) becomes small. Near \(\omega_d = \omega\) the amplitude peaks sharply, limited only by damping: resonance.

Keeping mirrors still. Resonance can destroy structures (pogo oscillations troubled early Saturn V flights). Telescopes and LIGO mount optics on soft supports with low natural frequencies, so that ground vibrations at much higher frequencies are suppressed by about \((\omega/\omega_d)^2\).

Orbital resonances. An asteroid whose period is a simple fraction of Jupiter's gets a periodic tug at the same orbital point; over millions of years this pumps up its eccentricity and clears it out. These Kirkwood gaps lie at

\[ a = a_J\left(\frac{P}{P_J}\right)^{2/3} \]

Resonances can also stabilize: Io, Europa and Ganymede are locked in the 1:2:4 Laplace resonance, which keeps Io's orbit eccentric so that tidal flexing (a forced, damped oscillation) heats it and powers over 400 volcanoes.

Key equations
\[ A = \frac{F_{max}}{\sqrt{(k - m\omega_d^2)^2 + b^2\omega_d^2}} \]
\[ a = a_J\left(\frac{P}{P_J}\right)^{2/3} \]

★Key takeaways

  • Periodic motion is described by amplitude \(A\), period \(T\), frequency \(f = 1/T\) and angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi f\).
  • A restoring force \(F = -kx\) gives simple harmonic motion, \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) with \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\), independent of amplitude; it is the projection of uniform circular motion.
  • Energy \(E = \tfrac12 kA^2\) is conserved and shared between \(K\) and \(U\); small oscillations about any stable equilibrium are simple harmonic.
  • A simple pendulum has \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\) and a physical pendulum \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{I/mgd}\); both swing more slowly where gravity is weaker.
  • Damping makes the amplitude decay as \(e^{-t/\tau}\); critical damping \(b_c = 2\sqrt{km}\) stops oscillation fastest, and \(Q\) measures how long a system rings.
  • Driving near the natural frequency causes resonance; orbital resonances with Jupiter carve the Kirkwood gaps and heat Io.

Example 14.3 Callisto as Galileo saw it

Callisto orbits Jupiter in a nearly circular orbit of radius \(1.883\times10^6\) km with period 16.69 days. Viewed edge on from Earth, its apparent position along Jupiter's equator undergoes SHM. Jupiter's radius is \(R_J = 71\,492\) km. (a) What is the amplitude in units of \(R_J\)? (b) Find \(\omega\), the maximum apparent speed and the maximum apparent acceleration. (c) If Callisto is at greatest eastern elongation (\(x = +A\)) at \(t = 0\), where is it 4.00 days later, and what is \(v_x\)?

Show solution (try it first)

Identify and set up: The projected motion is SHM with amplitude equal to the orbital radius and \(\omega\) equal to the orbital angular speed. Since \(x = +A\) at \(t = 0\), \(\phi = 0\): \(x = A\cos\omega t\), \(v_x = -\omega A\sin\omega t\).

Execute: (a) \(A/R_J = (1.883\times10^6\ \text{km})/(71\,492\ \text{km}) = 26.34\).

(b) With \(T = (16.69)(86\,400\ \text{s}) = 1.442\times10^6\ \text{s}\):

\[ \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 4.357\times10^{-6}\ \text{rad/s}, \quad v_{max} = \omega A = 8.205\ \text{km/s}, \quad a_{max} = \omega^2 A = 0.03575\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

(c) At \(t = 3.456\times10^5\ \text{s}\), \(\omega t = 1.506\ \text{rad}\). Then \(x = A\cos(1.506) = (1.883\times10^6\ \text{km})(0.06489) = 1.222\times10^5\ \text{km} = 1.71\,R_J\), and \(v_x = -(8.205\ \text{km/s})\sin(1.506) = -8.19\ \text{km/s}\).

Evaluate: Four days is just under a quarter period, so Callisto should be nearly in front of (or behind) Jupiter, moving at almost top speed toward \(-x\). That is what we found, and what Galileo's sketches show.

Example 14.9 A grandfather clock on the Moon and Mars

(a) A "seconds pendulum" has period 2.00 s. What length must it have on Earth (\(g = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\))? (b) A clock with an \(L = 0.994\) m pendulum is shipped to a lunar base (\(g = 1.62\ \text{m/s}^2\)) and a Mars base (\(g = 3.71\ \text{m/s}^2\)). What is its period at each site? (c) In one real Earth day, how many hours does each clock display?

Show solution (try it first)

Identify and set up: The gears count swings, assuming each takes 2.00 s. Use \(L = gT^2/4\pi^2\) for (a) and \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\) for (b). The displayed time is the real time multiplied by \(T_E/T = \sqrt{g/g_E}\).

Execute: (a) \(L = (9.80)(2.00)^2/4\pi^2\ \text{m} = 0.993\ \text{m}\).

(b) \(T_{Moon} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.994/1.62}\ \text{s} = 4.92\ \text{s}\); \(T_{Mars} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.994/3.71}\ \text{s} = 3.25\ \text{s}\).

(c) Moon: \((24.0\ \text{h})\sqrt{1.62/9.80} = 9.76\ \text{h}\). Mars: \((24.0\ \text{h})\sqrt{3.71/9.80} = 14.8\ \text{h}\).

Evaluate: Pendulum clocks are hopeless off Earth unless the pendulum is shortened by the factor \(g/g_E\); a lunar seconds pendulum would be only 0.164 m long. Spacecraft use quartz and atomic clocks, whose frequencies do not depend on gravity.

Example 14.14 The ringing Moon

After an impact, Apollo seismometers recorded the Moon ringing at a dominant frequency near 1.0 Hz. Take the quality factor of the lunar crust as \(Q = 3000\) and treat the ringing as a lightly damped oscillator. (a) What is the amplitude decay time \(\tau\)? (b) How long until the amplitude falls to 1.0% of its initial value? (c) How long until the energy falls by a factor of \(e\)?

Show solution (try it first)

Identify and set up: From \(Q = \omega\tau/2\), \(\tau = 2Q/\omega = Q/(\pi f)\). The amplitude varies as \(e^{-t/\tau}\), so it reaches a fraction \(F\) at \(t = \tau\ln(1/F)\). The energy goes as \(A^2\), i.e. as \(e^{-2t/\tau}\).

Execute: (a) \(\tau = 3000/(\pi\cdot1.0\ \text{Hz}) = 955\ \text{s}\).

(b) \(t = \tau\ln 100 = (955\ \text{s})(4.605) = 4.40\times10^3\ \text{s} = 73\ \text{min}\).

(c) The energy falls by \(e\) in \(\tau/2 = 477\ \text{s}\), about 8 minutes.

Evaluate: More than an hour of ringing is exactly what the Apollo records showed, astonishing seismologists used to earthquake records that die away in minutes. On Earth, water in rock pores raises the damping and lowers \(Q\) to a few hundred or less.
